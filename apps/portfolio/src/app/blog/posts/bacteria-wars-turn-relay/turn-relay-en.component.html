Bacteria Wars is a real-time strategy game in the browser: two colonies grow across a petri dish and fight for it, sixty ticks a second. Since October two players can fight each other online. That worked fine at my desk. Then players reported a familiar sentence: “Could not connect. Try again - on the same Wi-Fi it works best.” Two phones on mobile data could not play each other. And, the irony, neither could two devices on the same Wi-Fi.

This article is about the fix: a TURN relay. What problem it solves, what a TURN server is, how the game is wired to one without ever calling a backend function, and the one surprise that would have killed every relayed match once its credentials ran out: it took a test with one-minute credentials to see it in ten.

Peer to peer, in theory

An online match runs in lockstep: both devices run the same deterministic simulation with the same seed, and only the players’ inputs travel. Each input is played six ticks (100 ms) after it was made, on both devices, so it usually arrives before the other side needs it. That is a few dozen bytes sixty times a second; with both directions and the headers of UDP, DTLS and SCTP, about 6 MB for a ten-minute match. No game server needed: the two browsers talk directly over a WebRTC data channel.

To open that channel, the devices first have to swap a description of themselves (an SDP offer and answer, including every network address they could be reached at). The game does that through Firestore: the host writes its offer into a room document rooms/{code}, the guest reads it by the six-character code and writes its answer back. After that Firestore is out of the picture.

“Directly” is the catch. Most devices on the internet cannot be reached directly at all.

What a NAT does to an incoming packet

Your phone has a private address like 10.23.4.5. The internet sees the public address of a NAT (network address translation) in front of it: your router at home, or a carrier-grade NAT shared by thousands of phones on mobile data. When the phone sends a UDP packet out, the NAT picks a public port, remembers the mapping and lets the answers back in. A packet from anybody else to that port is dropped.

For two devices that each sit behind a NAT, that is a deadlock: the first packet of either one looks unsolicited to the other’s NAT. WebRTC solves it with hole punching: both learn their public address and port, swap them in the offer and answer, and send packets to each other at the same time. Each outgoing packet opens a mapping that lets the other’s packets in.

That works with most home routers. It fails in exactly the places players reported:

  • Symmetric and carrier-grade NATs on mobile networks hand out a new public port for every destination. The address a phone learned by asking a server is not the address the other phone would have to send to, so the punched hole is in the wrong place.
  • The same Wi-Fi should be the easy case, but browsers hide the local IP behind a random <uuid>.local name, which the other device has to resolve by multicast DNS. Mesh, guest and client-isolated networks block that. The fallback, the public address, needs the router to hairpin (send a packet to its own public address back into the LAN), and many home routers do not do that for UDP.

The game’s end-to-end tests never saw any of this: they ran both players in one Chromium on one machine, with the mDNS hiding switched off.

STUN and TURN

Two kinds of helper servers come with WebRTC, and their names sound more alike than their jobs are.

STUN (Session Traversal Utilities for NAT, RFC 8489) is a mirror. The device sends it one packet, and STUN answers with the address and port the packet came from: the public side of the NAT. It costs next to nothing, carries no game traffic and Google runs public ones. Until November that was all the game used.

TURN (Traversal Using Relays around NAT, RFC 8656) is a postman. The device asks it for an allocation: an address and port on the TURN server that is its own. Everything sent to that address is relayed to the device, everything the device sends through it goes out from there. Both NATs only ever see traffic to and from the TURN server, which they let through like any other outgoing connection. That works through every NAT that lets UDP or TLS out at all. The price: every packet of the match passes the server, and somebody pays for the bandwidth. That is why TURN needs credentials, and why it is the last resort.

How ICE picks a path

The browser does not choose between direct and relayed itself; ICE (Interactive Connectivity Establishment) does. While it prepares the offer or answer, the browser gathers candidates, addresses it might be reached at:

Candidate Where it comes from Works when
host the device’s own interface (as an mDNS name) both are on one network that resolves mDNS
srflx (server-reflexive) the public address STUN reported both NATs keep one public port for every destination (hole punching)
relay an allocation on the TURN server always, as long as the credentials are valid

Both sides send their candidates in the offer and answer. ICE then pairs them up and runs connectivity checks, cheapest pairs first, and nominates the best pair that answers. A relay pair has the lowest priority, so two devices that can talk directly still do. Only when nothing else gets through does the match run through the relay. One TURN server in the configuration is enough for that: no second negotiation, no “try direct, then relay” logic in the game.

Wiring it into the game

The game has a firm rule: the client never calls a function. It reads and writes Firestore documents, and Cloud Functions react to them. TURN credentials are usually handed out per user by a backend endpoint. Here they are shared and short-lived instead, and they live in one document.

I use Cloudflare’s Realtime TURN. A scheduled function, refreshRelay, asks Cloudflare every 30 minutes for credentials that are valid for an hour, and writes the TURN entry of the answer to relay/turn: 

{{ code.refresh }}

The document every player reads looks like this:

{{ code.relayDocument }}

A few details that are easy to get wrong:

  • Half the lifetime, at least. Valid for an hour, refreshed every half hour: whoever reads the document gets 30 minutes or more. A device keeps its copy while at least 20 minutes are left, so every new connection starts with 20 minutes at least. A failed refresh leaves the last credentials in place until they run out.
  • Flat storage. Cloudflare answers with an iceServers array of objects with urls arrays. Firestore in this project holds no arrays in arrays, so only the TURN entry is stored. URLs on port 53 are dropped: browsers block that port, and the attempt just times out.
  • Read, never list. The security rules let any signed-in, not banned player get the document. Nobody can list the collection or write to it; only the function does.
  • Never block a match. The game reads the document right before it builds the offer or the answer, gives up after 2 seconds and keeps a copy while more than 20 minutes are left. No document, an expired one, a slow read: the match connects with STUN only, as before.

On the device, the TURN entry simply joins Google’s STUN servers in the RTCPeerConnection. The transport itself knows nothing about Firestore; it gets the relay servers handed in: 

{{ code.peerConnection }}

The Cloudflare side is code, too

Nothing at Cloudflare is clicked together by hand. A small tool in the repo, tools-relay:turn-key, takes an account API token from a secret (CLOUDFLARE_API_TOKEN, the one manual input), makes sure a TURN key of a fixed name exists and stores the key as the secret CLOUDFLARE_TURN_KEY, the only one the function reads. A second run changes nothing. The key can only generate TURN credentials; it cannot touch the account.

The same tool sets up a spend alert. Cloudflare has no usage alert for TURN, but TURN is free up to a monthly allowance, so the first paid dollar of the account is the signal of misuse. And there is an off switch: RELAY_ON in the functions’ config takes the scheduler out of the next deploy, and within the hour the game is STUN-only again.

The link that died with its credentials

The first draft stopped there, and it would have shipped broken. Before the merge I wrote a check (tools-relay:check): two peer connections in one Playwright browser, forced through the relay with iceTransportPolicy: 'relay', credentials of only 60 seconds, and the channel held open for eleven minutes, past the moments Chrome refreshes its TURN permission (about every four minutes) and its allocation (its ten minute lifetime, less one).

  • No refresh: the channel dies once the credentials expired.
  • Fresh credentials through setConfiguration() every 30 seconds: it still dies. The browser keeps refreshing the old allocation with the old credentials.
  • Fresh credentials and an ICE restart: it lives.

Matches are short, but a lobby, a rematch and a second match easily outlast the 20 minutes a connection’s credentials have left at least. So the host renews the relay of an open link every 15 minutes. It takes fresh credentials, restarts ICE and sends the new offer, not through Firestore this time, but over a second negotiated data channel next to the game’s: 

{{ code.renewal }}

The game channel and the lockstep protocol stay untouched while ICE switches over underneath. Only the host offers, so the two sides never offer at the same time. A device of an older version has no signal channel and never answers; after 20 seconds the host rolls its offer back and the match goes on as before.

Lesson: a long-lived WebRTC connection over TURN is not done when it connects. Test it past the lifetime of its credentials and of the browser’s allocation, not just “does it connect”.

What it costs and what it leaks

  • Money. Cloudflare bills TURN egress beyond its free monthly allowance at $0.05 per GB. A relayed ten-minute match is about 6 MB. The risk is not the game, it is someone taking the shared credentials to relay their own traffic for up to an hour. The spend alert and the off switch are there for that.
  • Privacy. The browser gathers its relay candidate up front, so Cloudflare sees every online player’s IP address, ports and connection times at connection setup, relayed or not. The match itself stays encrypted end to end with DTLS; the relay forwards bytes it cannot read. The game’s privacy policy says so.
  • Per-player credentials were the alternative: a command document per player and a trigger that writes credentials back. Anonymous accounts cost nothing to create, so a limit per account gains little, and the trigger’s cold start would have delayed every first match.

Takeaways

  • Peer-to-peer without TURN works at your desk and fails on mobile data.
  • Add the TURN server to the ICE configuration and let ICE choose: direct paths stay direct.
  • Credentials do not need an endpoint: one shared document with a lifetime of twice its refresh interval is enough for a small game.
  • A relayed link outlives its credentials only with an ICE restart.

The decisions are written down in the repository: ADR 0009 (lockstep over WebRTC) and ADR 0020 (the TURN relay), and the whole story in issue #3148. Or skip the reading, grab a friend on mobile data and play a match.
